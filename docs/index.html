Quarterly income tax tracker and BIR Form 1701Q figure preparer for Philippine freelancers and virtual assistants

A single-binary tool that follows a freelancer's taxable year: record the fees as they come in, and it works out the cumulative tax due at the end of each quarter under the regime you elected, credits what the earlier quarters already paid, and lays the figures out ready to transcribe onto the return. It runs on your own computer and sends nothing anywhere. It prepares figures; it files nothing.

Solo VAs, freelance marketers, home-based workers Philippines · BIR NIRC secs. 74, 51, 56 & 116 · TRAIN law · BIR 1701Q / 1701 / 1701A

What it does

Registering with the Bureau of Internal Revenue is the part a Philippine freelancer is told about. The part nobody explains is what happens for the rest of the year: three quarterly income tax returns, each reporting the whole year to that point and crediting the tax the earlier quarters already paid, and then an annual return that does the same thing over the full twelve months. Get the crediting wrong and you either pay the same tax twice or file a figure you cannot support.

This tool does that one job properly:

  • records what came in — fees, retainers, platform payouts — and what went out, with the tax your clients withheld on their certificates;
  • works out, at the end of each quarter, the cumulative tax due on the year to date under the regime you elected, and subtracts the year-to-date credits: the prior year's excess, the tax the earlier quarters paid, and the withholding;
  • shows what is left to pay, or reports an overpayment in brackets as the return does, rather than handing you a negative payment;
  • keeps the quarterly percentage tax separate, because that return covers one quarter only and credits nothing — treating it like the income tax return is the commonest way to get a quarter wrong;
  • lays out the figures a quarterly or annual return asks for, named line by line, ready to transcribe;
  • gives you the year's filing dates with the provision each one comes from;
  • warns when gross passes the registration threshold, which ends the 8% election for the whole year, and works the year again under the graduated rates with what you already paid credited against it.

What it does not do, and why

It does not choose your regime. Whether the 8% flat rate or the graduated rates cost you less is a different question, answered once a year before you elect, and a separate tool in this family already answers it. Here the regime is an input: you tell this tool what you elected, and it tracks the year under it. We do not ship two tools for one job.

It also does not file, submit or e-file anything, it does not validate a return, and it does not reproduce the Bureau's form. What it produces is a named list of figures to copy onto the real return, on paper or in the Bureau's own filing software. And it does not compute a penalty: surcharge, interest and the compromise penalty on a late or incorrect return are for the Bureau to tell you.

Who it's for

Philippine self-employed individuals and professionals registered with the BIR who file their own returns: virtual assistants, freelance writers, designers and marketers, home-based customer-support workers, consultants and solo practitioners. It assumes you are keeping your own books in a spreadsheet or a notebook and want the quarterly arithmetic done without a bookkeeper.

It handles a mixed-income earner — somebody with a salary as well as freelance work — on the business side only, and says so: compensation income goes on the annual return, and a mixed-income earner gets no ₱250,000 reduction against business income under the 8% rate.

The rule it implements

The quarterly income tax return of an individual is cumulative. Under NIRC sec. 74(A), as amended by the TRAIN law (Republic Act No. 10963), an individual receiving self-employment income files a declaration of estimated income for the current taxable year on or before 15 May of the same taxable year — the date the TRAIN law moved from 15 April. Sec. 74(B) then provides that the tax is paid in four instalments: the first at the time of the declaration, and “the second and third shall be paid on August 15 and November 15 of the current year, respectively”, with the fourth “on or before April 15 of the following calendar year when the final adjusted income tax return is due to be filed”. That fourth instalment is the annual return under sec. 51(C)(1). There is no fourth quarterly return: the fourth quarter is settled on the annual one.

So each return reports the year to date and credits what came before it. The arithmetic this tool implements, for every quarter n, is:

taxable to date   = (income to date)  less (the deduction or allowance to date)
tax due to date   = the elected rate applied to the taxable income to date
credits to date   = prior year's excess
                  + tax PAID with the returns for quarters 1 .. n-1
                  + creditable tax withheld in quarters 1 .. n
                  + other credits
tax payable       = tax due to date  less  credits to date

   positive -> that is what you pay this quarter
   negative -> an overpayment, carried against the rest of the year

Two consequences fall out of that, and both are handled here rather than left to the reader. A loss quarter after a profitable one pulls the cumulative tax below what has already been paid, so the quarter produces an overpayment and nothing to pay — never a negative payment. And the figure credited for the earlier quarters is what you actually paid, not what the tool thinks you should have paid; where you have not told it, it says so on the figure itself.

The two regimes, as inputs

Under NIRC sec. 24(A)(2)(b), a self-employed individual whose gross sales or receipts do not exceed the value-added tax threshold, and who is not VAT-registered, may elect a flat 8% on gross sales, receipts and other non-operating income in excess of ₱250,000, in lieu of both the graduated income tax and the percentage tax. The election is signified on registration or on the first quarterly return of the year and is irrevocable for that taxable year. The ₱250,000 reduction belongs to an individual earning purely from business or a profession: BIR Form No. 1701 states it as “not applicable if with compensation income”.

Otherwise the graduated rates under sec. 24(A)(2)(a) apply to net income, with either itemised deductions or the 40% optional standard deduction under sec. 34(L) — which the return computes from sales net of returns, allowances and discounts, not from gross. Alongside it, a non-VAT person pays the quarterly percentage tax under sec. 116 on BIR Form No. 2551Q, within twenty-five days after the quarter ends (sec. 128(A)(1)). That return is per quarter and credits nothing.

This tool keeps a rate table per taxable year, because the law has moved twice within living memory of a freelancer's filing history: the graduated rates fell in 2023, and the percentage tax was cut to 1% from 1 July 2020 to 30 June 2023 by the CREATE law before returning to 3%. Both percentage-tax changes fell on the first day of a quarter, so no quarter has ever straddled two rates.

Crossing the threshold

If gross passes the ₱3,000,000 threshold during the year while you are on the 8% election, the election falls away for the whole year. Under Revenue Regulations No. 8-2018 the income tax is recomputed under the graduated rates, with the 8% already paid allowed as a credit, and registration must be updated within the month following the month in which the threshold was exceeded. This tool detects that, names the quarter, and works the year again the other way with what you paid credited — because at that point the figures on the face of your quarterly returns are no longer the ones to file.

Install & run

It is one Go program with no dependencies beyond the standard library, so there is nothing to install and nothing to configure.

git clone https://github.com/sreenivas-sadhu-prabhakara/freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot.git
cd freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot

# build a single binary
go build -o freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot .
./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot

# or just run it
go run .

Then open http://127.0.0.1:8080/ in a browser. Two flags change the defaults:

./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot -addr 127.0.0.1:9000 -data ~/tax/2026.json

It also runs once from the command line, which is what somebody filling in a return at the kitchen table wants:

# the year's position and the figures for one return
./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot -report -year 2026 -quarter 2

# every rate, threshold and filing date, with its statutory reference
./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot -constants

# this year's filing dates, and how far away each one is
./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot -deadlines

Entries can be typed one at a time or pasted in from a spreadsheet, one per line — date, amount, kind, note, tax withheld, expense category. Only the date and the amount are needed:

2026-02-15,25000,receipt,Acme February retainer,2500
2026-03-01,"12,500",receipt,Delta project
2026-03-04,750,refund,Delta overbilled
2026-03-08,2400,expense,Internet,,deduction

A line that cannot be read is reported by its number and left out. Nothing is guessed: an amount split across two fields by an unquoted thousands separator is named as such rather than half-read.

What it stores

One JSON file on your own computer, ./freelancer-va-tax-co-pilot-data.json by default, holding your entries, your election and any figures you have overridden — one record per taxable year, so last year stays where you left it. It is written atomically, to a temporary file that is then renamed, so an interrupted write cannot corrupt it, and it is created readable only by you.

Nothing else leaves the machine. The tool binds to 127.0.0.1, makes no outbound network calls of any kind, has no accounts, no analytics and no telemetry, and loads no fonts, scripts or styles from anywhere but itself. Your clients' names and your fees never go anywhere. Delete that one file and the tool knows nothing.

Features

Cumulative, with credits

Each quarter is the year to date, less the prior year's excess, less what the earlier quarters paid, less the withholding. The four payments add up to the year's tax exactly once.

An overpayment is an overpayment

A loss quarter, or a credit note, after a profitable quarter produces an overpayment carried forward and nothing to pay — never a negative payment on a return that has no field for one.

What you paid, not what we think

Record the payment you actually made with each return and the next quarter credits that. Until you do, the tool says on the figure itself that it is assuming its own.

The percentage tax kept apart

2551Q covers one quarter and credits nothing, unlike the income tax return beside it. The rate is read for the quarter, so 2020 and 2023 come out right.

Figures, named line by line

Grouped the way the return groups them, rounded to whole pesos as the return asks, with the exact centavos still shown. Item numbers only where they could be sourced.

The threshold, before it bites

It names the quarter gross passed ₱3,000,000, works the year again under the graduated rates with the 8% credited, and says what registration has to change.

Every figure in one place

Each rate, threshold and filing date is a named constant with the provision it comes from, published in the interface and by -constants, and every one of them is yours to override.

Amounts in whole centavos

No floating point anywhere. A year of entries adds up exactly, and the rounding to pesos follows the return's own rule: 49 centavos down, 50 up.

Rates, thresholds and dates used — all indicative, all overridable
  • 8% income tax rate, on gross sales, receipts and other non-operating income in excess of the allowance, in lieu of the graduated income tax and the percentage tax — NIRC sec. 24(A)(2)(b), as amended by the TRAIN law (R.A. No. 10963).
  • ₱250,000 allowable reduction under the 8% option, for an individual earning purely from business or a profession — NIRC sec. 24(A)(2)(b); stated on BIR Form No. 1701 (January 2018 ENCS) at Part V schedule 3.B item 29 as “not applicable if with compensation income”. It is deducted once against the year to date, not once a quarter.
  • ₱3,000,000 VAT registration threshold, which is also the ceiling on electing the 8% rate and on paying the percentage tax instead of VAT — NIRC secs. 109(1)(BB) and 116, as amended by the TRAIN law.
  • Graduated rates, 2018 to 2022: nil to ₱250,000 at nil; then 20%, 25%, 30%, 32% and 35% over ₱250,000, ₱400,000, ₱800,000, ₱2,000,000 and ₱8,000,000 — NIRC sec. 24(A)(2)(a), TRAIN law first schedule.
  • Graduated rates, 2023 onwards: the same bands at 15%, 20%, 25%, 30% and 35% — the TRAIN law's second schedule. A year is taxed on the table that applied to it.
  • 40% optional standard deduction of sales net of returns, allowances and discounts — NIRC sec. 34(L); BIR Form No. 1701 item 17 computes it from net sales, not from gross.
  • Percentage tax: 3% of the quarter's gross sales or receipts under NIRC sec. 116; 1% from 1 July 2020 to 30 June 2023 under sec. 13 of the CREATE law (R.A. No. 11534); back to 3% from 1 July 2023 per BIR RMC No. 69-2023.
  • Filing dates: the quarterly income tax return on 15 May, 15 August and 15 November of the taxable year (NIRC secs. 74(A) and 74(B)); the annual return on 15 April of the following year (sec. 51(C)(1)); the percentage tax return within 25 days after each quarter ends (sec. 128(A)(1)).
  • Two instalments: where the annual tax due exceeds ₱2,000, an individual may elect to pay in two equal instalments, the second on or before 15 October following the close of the calendar year — NIRC sec. 56(A)(2) as amended by the TRAIN law; BIR Form No. 1701 item 25 allows 50% or less of the tax due to be deferred.
  • An individual's taxable year is the calendar year (NIRC sec. 43), so the quarters are always January–March, April–June, July–September and October–December, and no fiscal-year option is offered.
  • Rounding: amounts are held in whole centavos and rounded to pesos only for transcription, by the return's own rule — “49 Centavos or Less drop down; 50 or more round up”.
What could not be sourced, and what it means for you
  • The item numbers of BIR Form No. 1701Q could not be sourced from a published copy of the form. The quarterly figures are therefore named and not numbered — match them to your copy of the return by their labels. The item numbers offered for BIR Form No. 1701 were read from the form itself (January 2018 ENCS).
  • BIR Form No. 1701A was not obtained either. The tool names it as the return to use when you are purely self-employed and on the 8% rate or the optional standard deduction, and it lists the same figures without item numbers.
  • The ₱3,000,000 threshold is subject to a three-yearly adjustment to present value using the consumer price index under the TRAIN law. No adjustment to this threshold had been issued at the time of writing, so it stands at three million pesos — but confirm it, and override it here if it has moved.
  • A deadline falling on a weekend or a public holiday is flagged, not moved. This tool states the statutory date and tells you when it lands on a Saturday or a Sunday; what the working date then is, is for the Bureau to say. Philippine public holidays are not encoded at all.
  • Penalties are not computed. The Ease of Paying Taxes Act (R.A. No. 11976) reduced the surcharge and the interest for micro and small taxpayers, but the compromise penalty is a schedule and the interest runs from facts this tool does not hold. Ask the Bureau what is due on a late return.
  • The basis of your records is your own. Whether you record a fee when you invoice it or when it is collected determines what the figures mean; the Ease of Paying Taxes Act moved VAT and the percentage tax onto gross sales. This tool totals what you give it and does not second-guess it.
  • The percentage tax base used here is the quarter's sales net of returns, allowances and discounts. Other non-operating income is not a sale or receipt of the business and is left out of it. If you have non-operating income, confirm the treatment.
This tool works out figures from what you record. It does not check them against your books, your invoices or your clients' certificates, it does not file, submit or validate anything, it does not compute penalties, and it is not tax, accounting or legal advice. Confirm every rate, threshold and filing date with the Bureau of Internal Revenue or a qualified practitioner before you file.
